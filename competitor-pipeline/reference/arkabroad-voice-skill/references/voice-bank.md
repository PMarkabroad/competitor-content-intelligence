## Contrarian inversions

> The jobs you actually do in corporate Australia are not that hard as you're supposed to think. And the people you think work in corporate Australia — you think they're so smart, like the boogeyman. They're not. They're ordinary people who positioned themselves correctly, who know how to carry themselves, who know how to speak. And they get the roles.

> Yes, there is racism. There are going to be people who hate you for the colour of your skin, for your accent. But if you're charging someone $70,000 a year and giving them $150,000 of value, that objection disappears. He doesn't care if you're Korean or Indian. He cares about the revenue.

> It's not like you study data analytics, so you'll be a data analyst.

**Pattern:** name the belief in the audience's own words → flat contradiction → the mechanism in one sentence.

## The empathy pivot

> The ones who are trying every day to change their lives around, but nothing seems to be moving the needle for them. They're taking action. They're doing everything right. They're executing like crazy. They're working hard in their survival jobs. They're managing their expenses, they're managing their family's expectations, they're paying the student loan back home. They're doing so many things right. They're hard workers. But they still don't get the role they came here for and the role they actually deserve.

> They take so much in loans to come to this country. They have so much hope from their family. A lot of them have good experience, they're very smart. But they're still working survival jobs.

> I'm not saying working in retail or warehousing is bad. But if you've spent upwards of $200,000 to get a degree and after that you're working a survival job — that is not good.

**Pattern:** the disclaimer ("I'm not saying X is bad") is load-bearing. It buys permission for the harder line that follows. Keep it.

## Subdivision teaching

> What kind of data analyst will you be? Will you be more towards the governance side? More towards the extraction, transforming, cleaning side? More towards the dashboarding and the insights side? Nobody tells you that. A person who is highly organised and introverted, who studied data, is going to be a better data governance analyst. Similarly, a person who has very good visual taste and an ability to talk to people — they will make a great reporting or dashboarding analyst.

> In software engineering there's the person who tests the bugs, there's the person who is more of a creator. In business analysis there's the person who does wireframing, process mapping, process optimisation. Within a specific role there are so many sub-roles. And those sub-roles are meant for different personality types.

**Pattern:** three or four named sub-roles → "nobody tells you that" → personality-to-sub-role mapping. This template ports to any profession.

## Anger at systems

> The career services in universities is an absolute scam. They don't even tell you the kind of roles you should be applying for based on your inherent strengths.

> A university in the Group of Eight doesn't teach you anything about LinkedIn. It doesn't teach you anything about a project portfolio. It teaches you absolutely nothing about a resume.

> During my degree I learned SPSS and Stata. Nothing of it is used now. I had to learn everything myself.

> There was a lot of misinformation — about visa status, about how the workplace is racist towards Indians, about how you can't get a role. And I bought into that advice, because that was the only free advice available online.

**Note:** this is the single strongest emotional register in the voice. It works because Arka is angry *on behalf of* the reader, not at them.

## Repetition and rhythm

> I keep on applying. I keep on applying. Nobody wants to listen to me. Nobody wants to talk to me.

> I learn operations. I learn how a script is made. I learn GTM systems. In one year I learned what I couldn't have learned in four.

> I have applied to a thousand, two thousand places with no replies at all.

> I refuse to give up. I lose. I stay down on the ground long enough. But I am going to stand back up.

**Pattern:** three beats, then break the pattern on the fourth. The break is where the meaning lands.

## Origin-story openers

> So when I was finding a job, there was a very big gap in the market. There was no advice on how international students can actually place themselves.

> Imagine being 18 years old, moving to a new country, and the past year you've sat at home with no social interaction because of COVID.

> My friends used to come to me for advice. How do we get the role? How do we make a resume? You got two offers — please explain how this happened.

**Pattern:** drop the reader into a scene mid-motion. No scene-setting, no "let me tell you about the time."

## Signature constructions

| Construction | Example |
|---|---|
| "Nobody tells you that." | Closer after revealing a sub-role or mechanism. |
| "That is not good." | Flat, understated verdict after a heavy setup. More powerful than an intense one. |
| "I'm not saying X is bad. But—" | Buys permission before a hard line. |
| "It's a simple logic." | Closes a contrarian argument. Signals the reader should feel it's obvious now. |
| "What can I do as a person to help them?" | Mission framing — always phrased as personal obligation, never as market opportunity. |
| "$X to $Y" | Never a single figure when a range is more concrete. |
| "The role you came here for." | The core promise phrase. Use it. |
